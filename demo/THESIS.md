# What the demo says, and in whose voice (2026-11-02 12:50)

Eric's question: in all of this, what problems does the demo actually express, how does it show them solved, and
how does it sound like it comes from someone who does this work — not from a quant who wandered in?

## The problems the demo shows, and the catch that answers each

| The problem the owner lives with | Why it happens | What Landsight does on screen | What the viewer concludes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The package is unreviewable as delivered.** Thirty files: backups, an Excel 97 quote, a zip, a scan, a deck, four bill revisions. Nothing opens cleanly; nobody knows which file governs. | Developers forward whatever their consultants had; nobody curates. | Every file gets a verdict and a reason in seconds; the governing bill and the governing revision are named; junk says why it is junk. | "It can take the real folder." |
| **The drawings don't say what they seem to.** A tree is twelve loose lines; a label carries a species to seven others; a hedge is a note; one sheet repeats another from the side; an enlargement shows four trees again; the title block lies about the scale. | CAD habits: exploded blocks, "typ.", match lines, copied sheets, out-of-date title blocks. | Geometry closes and measures what it can; Muse Spark reads the rest and says why; counts are reconciled against the schedule; sheets that repeat are named and excluded; nothing is counted until a person confirms. | "It reads the drawing the way an estimator does, and it shows its work." |
| **The bill drifts from the drawing.** Twelve billed, eight drawn; a hedge billed by the metre; a unit rate far from the firm's own; a subtotal that skips a row; a hidden sheet. | Bills are edited by hand across revisions; nobody re-counts. | Rows aligned to drawing items; three amber flags; the money in dollars; the trace bill → schedule → drawing. | "It finds the money." |
| **The rules nobody checks until the permit office does.** Greening short under Art. 302; a tree-pit detail that downgrades every large tree; a fire lane; a protected tree marked for removal; a water-retention index nobody computed. | The code is in Chinese articles, applied by the architect late, with inputs scattered across sheets. | Required vs provided, the article number, the two fixes, the designer's lever (a species change), the conditional cases flagged for the architect. | "It catches the thing that would have cost the permit." |
| **The PDFs you are expected to read yourself.** Specs, review issues, standards, the green-building checklist. | They arrive as documents, not data. | Ask the package: a question, an answer, the file and the sentence it came from, a button that turns it into a confirmed element, price or note. | "It read the package so I don't have to — and I can check it." |

The through-line: **every number traces back to a mark on a drawing or a sentence in a document.** That is the
promise, stated at 0:08 and paid at the scoreboard.

## Whose voice

Not a quant's. The v1 close ("I'm a quant, I don't trust a number I can't trace") was a non sequitur to the
judges and false to the story. Two honest options:

1. **The builder** (first person, Eric): "I built this to give a firm's owner her weeks back." Credible, and it
   never mentions trading. Everything after that line is said as someone who knows the job.
2. **The owner's voice** (second person): "You get the package on a Friday…" — the film addresses the owner
   directly, and Eric appears only on the end card as the builder.

Recommendation: option 1 for the cold open and the close (eight words each), option 2's "you" for the body.
Nothing in the narration refers to finance, quant work or trading. The expertise on screen is the estimator's:
"typ." carries, not multiplies; the plan governs the schedule; a pit under four square metres makes a large
tree small; the deduction belongs to the architect.

## What this changes for script v5

- The cold open is the folder and the owner's three weeks, then the bill's 12 against the drawing's 8.
- Each scenario is one scene with one reveal, in the order the owner meets them: format → design → bill →
  regulation → ask the package.
- The voice is the builder-who-sat-with-the-firm; the on-screen words are the estimator's.
- The stack is fifteen seconds, late, for the judges: "Muse Spark reads what geometry can't close and every
  document in the folder; SAM 3.1 measures the colour plan; every call is recorded and replayable."

## If Eric opens the film (how to show him, and what he says)

Judges in stage three like to see a builder once; nobody wants a founder selling at them. So: hands first,
face once, voice throughout.

- **Where the camera is.** Overhead or three-quarter, on a real desk: a laptop with the package folder open,
  a printed L-1 sheet, a highlighter, a cold coffee. Window light from one side; no ring light, no bookshelf
  backdrop, no product in frame yet. Phone on a tripod, 4K 30 fps, exposure and focus locked, two takes.
- **Beat 1 (0:00–0:06), hands only.** His hand drags the folder onto the laptop (thirty file icons spill); the
  other hand flips the printed sheet, the highlighter ticks eight canopies. Voice, dry, 110 wpm: *"A developer's
  package. Thirty files. The owner of the landscape firm reviews it by hand — three weeks, every time."*
- **Beat 1b (0:06–0:09), face, once.** Medium shot, eye level, slightly off-centre, dark plain shirt, looking at
  the camera for one line only: *"I sat next to her while she did it. I built this to give her the three weeks
  back."* Cut on "back" to the app. No name, no title, no smile-sell; the end card carries his name.
- **The body (0:09–2:45), voice only.** Everything is the app; his voice narrates as the person who watched the
  job done. The on-screen words are the estimator's.
- **Close (2:50–3:00), face, once more.** Same shot, three seconds: *"Every number traces back to the drawing.
  Now she can check it."* End card.
- **Delivery.** Flat, unhurried, no "hi I'm Eric", no adjectives. The flatter the better next to the reveals.
  If the takes are not flatter and better timed than the ElevenLabs read, keep the read and use his face only.
- **Fallbacks.** Hands-only with the ElevenLabs voice (no face at all) also works; the typed row remains the
  no-camera version.
- **The X cut (20 s).** Hands + the drop + the gauge + the scoreboard, text on screen, no face needed.

## The retention ladder (Eric, 13:10): an outsider must want the next 2, 5, 15 and 60 seconds

The judges are not landscape people, and neither is anyone who sees the X cut. So the first minute is built for
someone who has never seen a drawing, with no jargon ("DXF", "Art. 302", "take-off") before 1:00 — "the plan",
"the building code", "the bill".

| Checkpoint | What keeps them | The device on screen |
|---|---|---|
| **+2 s** | motion and mess everyone recognises | a hand drags a folder; it bursts into thirty files that start sorting themselves |
| **+5 s** | a count anyone can check | a spreadsheet says 12; a hand counts 8 on paper; **12 ≠ 8** — the open loop |
| **+15 s** | stakes a layperson feels, a promise, and the first pretty thing | "$11,000 and a permit"; "thirty errors are hidden in this folder"; the sheet wakes up — outlines light, numbers tick |
| **+60 s** | three small magic tricks, each 10–12 s, each with a question that pulls to the next | the eye (the machine looks at twelve lines, says "one tree — because…", and still asks permission); the tape (a note becomes 182 plants); the colour pour (lines → colour → masks bloom) |
| **after 1:00** | twists and money | the pit detail kills the obvious fix; the bill traced two hops back; the hidden sheet slides out; the scoreboard grades itself, misses included |

Rules for the cut:
- Something lands every 5–7 s; a new question is asked each time one is answered.
- Text on screen for every landing; the film works muted.
- Hands and a face in the first nine seconds; the product only after the promise.
- No feature is named; only what it catches. The stack is fifteen seconds near the end.
- The X cut is the ladder's first 20 s with the scoreboard spliced in.

Test before shooting: the script v5 judge panel includes two outsiders — a 25-year-old who watches short video
all day and a venture investor who gives a film eight seconds — asked only "at 2, 5, 15 and 60 seconds, do you
keep watching, and why".
